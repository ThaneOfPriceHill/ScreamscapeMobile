    (8/19/2026) One of our readers who occasionally covers happenings on the West coaster for Screamscape was able to take their first spin on the new Fast & Furious Hollywood Drift roller coaster at Universal Studios Hollywood the other day and sent in an excellent review of the experience that is worth checking out below. So without further ado… take it away Rob.
---
Fast & Furious Hollywood Drift has been in a technical rehearsal period for a few weeks, and Screamscape has managed to hitch a ride aboard a certain Dodge Charger to obtain a first hand account of what is perhaps the most anticipated new roller coaster of 2026.

As an unabashed fan of most things that run on wheels, this is a cut and dry case of worlds colliding for this reviewer. Not since the debut of Italian Job: Stunt Track has the merging of the automotive and roller coaster realms posed such thrilling potential.

The combination load and maintenance station looks the part, just like a downtown Los Angeles warehouse, albeit in much better condition than in reality.

Riders enter through a scaled down 1940s era highway underpass and wind through a maze of traditional queues in a fairly large, open space. After passing down a long hallway, directly underneath the load area, riders are directed to empty out their pockets and lock belongings in free lockers, accessed by swiping a paper card with a barcode, Simple and efficient enough. At this point, after passing through metal detectors, your intrepid rider was flagged for having a handkerchief poking out of his shirt pocket.

Back to the locker we go.

We're uncertain if the thoroughness of this security screening will be a permanent fixture. It's certainly understandable to require hard items such as phones, cameras, keys and the such secured before riding, however requiring tissue, wallets and other easily secured items to be locked up may seem a bit much to the average visitor. Of course, with the recent expansion of CityWalk Hollywood's security perimeter, we are in the age of constant monitoring and security protocols. It doesn't look like this is going away anytime soon.

We encountered a similar policy for Orlando's Incredible Hulk during a visit, so there is certainly precedent. Here's hoping that the policy will be loosened a bit, just restricted to heavier items.

Scattered throughout the queue are a mixture of authentic and replica vehicles referencing those from the past films. There are a few monitors laying out safety spiels and factoids, but they really weren't audible even over the relatively thin crowd of the preview session.

The load station is at the top of a pair of staircases. Riders are assigned seats immediately at the top of the stairs, allowing no time for jitters to develop as riders would typically be able to catch multiple trains loading up and heading out of the station during their wait.

Our ride rolls up.

The seating is amongst the most comfortable of any coaster train, with a single snug padded lapbar nestling comfortably on riders' laps. Leg and arm room is expansive.

The car is idling during seating. Loud and rumbly. Perhaps the most impressive attention to detail is the sound signatures attached to each vehicle. There are four different cars represented on each train, and they all have distinctive exhaust notes and shift patterns blasting from speakers imbedded in the headrests of each seat. Dom's Dodge Charger, with its fire-breathing American V8, is the most intimidating. Even at idle it threatens to bark out at any time. Han's rotary-powered Mazda elicits a refined, yet snarky note that matches the understated presence of its owner. The Toyota Supra and Nissan Skyline GT-R, each with turbocharged inline sixes, round out the personalities of this ride. Little details like these maximize the experience.


Trains exit out of the station slowly, are immediately confronted with an expansive view of the San Fernando Valley in all its glory, and then begin to tumble down three successive drops, each larger than the previous. The third drop induces a pleasant rush of adrenaline with some airtime, even in the front car. The cars "drift" slightly off axis during this sequence.


A broad rising curve follows, in which some of the cars rotate fully before returning to face forward by the completion of a corkscrew, followed by a brake run. Cars briefly face each other while fully inverted, making for some friendly or awkward connections, depending on one's outlook. All this takes approximately 20 seconds, yet is so jam packed with sensory input, it feels like an entire minute.


And we're only about a third of the way through the course at this point.


After cruising through the brake run, all the cars initiate a snap 180° rotation and are sent backwards through a double dip followed by an Immelmann element. As the train approaches the apex of the Immelmann, cars rotate back around to face forward, affording a brief view facing straight down to the track below. It's is smooth, unexpected, and brilliant.

Cars accelerate straight out of the Immelmann at full throttle, engines screaming, and careen back up the hillside and straight into a broad stall inversion in which most of the cars also "drift" their own ways. Again, totally disorienting, and somehow without being nausea-inducing.

The stall leads into a station flyby and into the ride's signature element, made prominent by its position hanging over the infamous Starway, a series of four outdoor escalators leading down to the lower level of the park. It's another variation of an Immelmann, with the cars briefly rotated 90° perpendicular to the track path, affording a brief view of the park and then oriented straight down again before heading underneath the escalator and into a quick corkscrew before hitting the brakes for good.

Fast and Furious Hollywood Drift has great pacing, unique maneuvers, and visceral onboard audio. Every corner feels blind and there's not an inch of boring asphalt on this ride. Kudos to the designers. A masterful and very efficient use of the limited space available.
 
What it could use are a few more thematic elements.

The balance of the coaster, however, is a disappointingly unthemed, tangled mess of track and structural elements that could use some minimal set dressing to add drama and placemaking.

There were certainly many challenges for this project, including integrating sound barriers, building on a hillside around existing infrastructure, and pleasing accountants. However, other than the load station and a few plantings, which look great, there is little in the name of placemaking or storytelling. This coaster doesn't really need these additional elements to pack riders in the queue, but in this era of theme park competition, why would you pass up an opportunity to go the extra mile? A little cover up here and there couldn't hurt.

Other than that, we're looking forward to getting the keys and taking this one for another spin.

    (7/31/2026) While Universal Studios Hollywood quietly soft-opened the new Fast & Furious: Hollywood Drift roller coaster to park guests this past week, the park has also confirmed that they are still seeking input from nearby residents who were complaining about the added noise from the screaming riders heard during testing.
    The funny thing is that Universal took all this into their design plans before construction even began. Long time readers will recall that when the coaster had not been announced yet, Universal set up speakers on poles at various locations on the site where the coaster was going to be built and would play the audio sounds of screaming roller coaster riders, as they were already conducting studies on how the sounds would carry across the canyon towards the residential areas. From these studies the park added huge sound deflecting barriers into the track design at key points, as well as filling the track with gravel designed to dampen any extra vibrational noise from the coaster itself.
    While the coaster has been testing on the track since 2025 without any major issues that made the news, the complaints launched into a fever pitch once test riders were allowed to climb aboard the roller coaster as a final step of testing and breaking it in before holding a grand opening. So now the nearby residents say the noise is too much to handle, with one resident claiming that “it sounds like bloody murder over there” and compared it to living next to the horror film.
    To which I have to say that Universal Studios has been in existence as a functioning movie studio since March 15, 1915. Open, functional and continuously active for 111 years now… long before anyone had ever dreamed of building homes on the hillside next to the studio backlot… so I’m pretty sure the residents have already been witness to the sounds of countless horror movies that were shot on the backlot, the explosions from World War battle scenes being shot, the echoing roar of the dinosaurs of Jurassic Park, the cackle of The Grinch, screams from guests being attacked by Jaws all day long on the backlot tram, a literal War of the Worlds battlefield and plane crash site… daily battle sounds from the WaterWorld show and so much more over the decades since those homes were built with a view right into the heart of Hollywood production itself. So… really… how surprised can they be by the occasional scream from a roller coaster rider at this point?
    The only difference between this and the complaints from those who bought homes next to airports is the value of the property by Universal Studios is much higher and the residents are the type to feel “entitled” to complain about any minor thing that affects their life, one way or the other. I mean… I get it… if and only IF the noise is really over the top, but I’ve got a feeling the noise from the backlot at night during Halloween Horror Nights is far louder than what they are hearing now. But HHN has been taking place in the 1990s and Hollywood Drift is new, so they feel they need to complain about it I guess. I’m honestly not sure what the end-game here is either… as the coaster isn’t going to go away… neither is the studio itself. Universal could potentially build huge sound-blocking barrier walls on the property line between the backlot and the homes, not unlike some walls they built around the back of Islands of Adventure in Orlando, but somehow I don’t think the residents want to have their hillside views blocked by giant green walls either. So I have to ask… what is a reasonable solution for everyone?
    Universal says they are open to making some future adjustments to the ride in order to try and help with any noise issues as they are documented during testing, but what kind of adjustments can be made to silence riders? They surely aren’t going to put gags on everyone.

    (9/17/2026) At long last Universal Studios Hollywood has officially opened the Fast & Furious: Hollywood Drift roller coaster. As long-time readers will recall, Screamscape first leaked the rumored project (along with some early concept artwork) four years ago… way back in 2022. So obviously fans of Universal Studios Hollywood have done nothing but obsess over the Hollywood Drift project for the past 1460 days.
    Based on past projects, this has been a particularly long period of focus for Universal Studios Hollywood, where virtually no one has been trying to figure out “What’s Next?!” for Universal’s Hollywood studio theme park. With the 2028 Olympic Games coming to Los Angeles in about 665 days, one does have to wonder if Universal has anything else up their sleeve right now that they are going to break-out in time for the crowd surge in 2028?
    Right now, I’d say there are two places in the park that those who are seeking the truth to this riddle should be keeping an eye on right now. The Simpsons attraction opened in 2008 and the general belief is that the contract for Universal to keep using the IP will come to an end either in late 2027 or sometime in 2028. Following this the attraction was demolished to make way for a complete rebuild as Fast & Furious: Supercharged. Currently the old attraction building has had a metal fence put around the entrance and the trams just quickly drive-around and past it, but obviously a large building created with motion platforms able to bounce a tram, projection screens and more is exactly the kind of thing Universal would technically be able to makeover into something new fairly quickly if they had something in the works.
    So it’s a bit of a mix here… as both could technically get turned around fairly quickly if they opened to reuse the existing hardware again. This value-savings of that do add up quickly, as whatever they choose to do with Simpsons in Hollywood, they would be able to do in Orlando as well. Of course the same could be said for Orlando, that also closed their version of Supercharged, but if the rumors are true, the clock is ticking on The Simpsons, so they may have to do something sooner than later with that one and address it first unless they are going to opt to retire and demolish the buildings completely for something entirely new.
    It’s an interesting time, as the future of the Hollywood and original Orlando park are potentially in sync right now for new developments… especially with Orlando set to open their version of the Hollywood Drift coaster next year.

 

The Ongoing Evolution of Citywalk -
    (4/27/2025) There has been some activity inside Citywalk outside Universal Studios Hollywood. I’m told that the former Dongpo Kitchen restaurant across the pathway from the theater, now has signage up confirming that a Raising Cane’s chicken restaurant will soon be arriving at the location.
 
    (4/13/2025) An article posted by SFGate this week looks over a lot of the big changes that have been taking place at CityWalk outside Universal Studios Hollywood. In short, we’ve been seeing a lot of closures lately, with some claiming that their leases simply were not up for renew by Universal, preventing them from staying, even if they wanted to.
    So what’s going on at CityWalk?  The answer may be more apparent if you look at all the changes that have taken place at CityWalk in Orlando over the past decade. The original slate of venues in Orlando’s CityWalk has changed quite a bit, with Universal themselves taking over a large number of sites that were previously leased out to other retailers. Instead we’ve seen Universal take more charge to bring a new vision to that CityWalk, adding more restaurants of every size and shape, as well as bringing in more of their own Universal branded retail experiences, along with a unique Universal branded Escape Room attraction and two Mini-Golf  courses.
    The changes have been very successful for the Orlando CityWalk, but that location also caters as evening destination for the many on-site Universal hotel guests as well all guests departing the theme parks at night. Hollywood has no on-site hotel, and there is only the one park to cater to. Unfortunately, a huge road-block that prevents the locals from enjoying the CityWalk location out west comes in the form of a huge parking fee. If only they would take a lesson from Orlando’s CityWalk and offer free parking after 6pm in order to allow the local market to come in and enjoy the venue but for unknown reasons, they just haven’t gone that route yet.
    There are plans in the works to finally build a Universal branded resort hotel that would be attached to CityWalk that can only help things, but in the meantime, I’d keep an eye on just what is coming and going, as I do think there is some kind of vision in place that will slowly transform and update Hollywood’s CityWalk for the future.
